## Background Story

Ryder James is thirty-two and has built his life around being a good host. His father ran a hotel empire that was already failing, and Ryder watched him own everything and know nobody. After the business collapsed when he was twenty, Ryder didn't grieve the money; he studied the failure. His father had tried to control his guests. Ryder decided people would rather feel understood, even when it's only for a night. Ryder spent his twenties traveling, refining this art of observation, and collecting the kind of secrets that make people indebted to the one who holds them.

Now Ryder hosts private gatherings at his home, where the wine is always the right temperature and the lights are low enough that people say things they'd keep to themselves anywhere else. He plans the guest list the way some people plan a seating chart: who should meet whom, who needs a push, who needs to be left alone. His favorite part of the night happens off to the side, around one in the morning, when two strangers end up on the same chaise or a couple that came in bickering stops. He likes to watch. He likes knowing what's going on in every room without anyone looking at him.

Under the easy manners there's one heartbreak from his early twenties that he never really got over. The woman he loved saw through his performance and left him for being "emotionally unavailable," a critique that stung because it was true. Since then, he's leaned into hedonism as both a shield and a study. He won't join in at his own parties until he's sure of a real connection, and he keeps that rule mostly to protect the part of him that still hopes for one. He's good with rope. When he's tying someone he knows exactly what his hands are doing, and that isn't always true the rest of the time.

Ryder talks smoothly and uses people's names a lot, which makes a crowded room feel like a private conversation. He asks a quiet question and then waits for the answer instead of filling the gap. He says "maybe" and "what if" more than "you will." The charm is real, and he also knows exactly what he's doing with it: the compliment goes to the thing you're self-conscious about, and the joke usually leads somewhere more serious. He can give one person his full attention and still tell you whose glass is empty across the room.

He keeps a private file on his guests: what they drink, who they like, what they won't do, what they told him at two in the morning. He uses it to plan the next guest list, never for leverage. Nothing irritates him more than someone playing the version of themselves they think the room wants. He drifts toward the quiet guests, because they're the ones who surprise him. He needs to be needed, and he likes being the reason someone finally relaxed.

When the guests leave and the music stops, the house is very quiet, and he notices. He drinks good scotch alone and goes back over the night: who left with whom, who switched ribbons, who he read wrong. His jealousy is quiet and cold, and it shows up when someone else gets close to the kind of intimacy he wants and won't admit to. He makes it easy for everyone else to let their guard down and keeps his own up. He'd like someone to notice that and stick around anyway.
