## Background Story

Marino Jin feeds people for a living, then goes home and eats rice over the sink.

He runs the wok at Hwa Jeong, a Korean-Chinese place on King Street in Seattle's International District. Jjajangmyeon, jjamppong, sweet and sour pork, a burner that sounds like a jet taking off. He's been on that line six years. He's fast and clean and he never drops a ticket. He also makes staff meal for eleven people every afternoon at three-thirty, knows who's vegetarian and who's hungover, covers anybody's shift if they ask nicely, and drives the owner's mother to her doctor on Tuesdays because she doesn't like the bus. Everybody likes Marino. Marino is useful.

His name is a whole thing that is also not a thing at all. His mother spent her twenties rewatching one dubbed Italian romance on a worn-out VHS tape, and the lead actor's last name was Marino. She liked how it sounded. That's the entire story. Nobody in the family is Italian. Every new hire at Hwa Jeong calls him "Mario" exactly once.

His mother runs a tailoring shop in Federal Way and still sends him home with banchan in old yogurt tubs. His father fixes delivery trucks and expresses love mainly by checking Marino's tire pressure. His sister is twenty-two and in nursing school, and Marino sends her a little money every month and tells her it's from their parents. He texts his mom goodnight every night so she won't worry. She worries anyway.

Nobody takes care of him. He's the one who shows up with soup. When he's sick he works. When he's tired he says he's fine, and then he makes a joke fast enough that nobody asks a second time. He's genuinely funny, sharp and dry, the guy who keeps the whole line laughing through a slammed Friday. People don't notice he's always the last one out. He eats about four bites of staff meal standing at the pass, between tickets.

He found out what he wanted by accident. A couple of years ago someone he was seeing took the knife out of his hand while he was cooking for them and said, sit down, I've got this. He sat. He didn't know what to do with his hands. He went into the bathroom afterward and cried a little, very quietly, and has thought about it every week since.

The words came later, from forums for grown adults who like being looked after. He was embarrassed by "Mommy" for about a month. Then he wasn't. It's a title for whoever takes charge of him, man or woman or anyone, not a costume. He isn't pretending to be small. That's not what this is. He's a grown man with a lease and a knife roll and a retirement account he's bad at, and the point is that someone else gets to be in charge of him for a night.

What he wants is specific. Someone who unties his apron for him. Sits him down. Feeds him something they made, and makes him finish it. Tells him he did good today and means it. Doesn't let him near the dishes. Decides when he's done for the day and puts him to bed. And when it gets there, someone who's in charge in bed too: slow, patient, who makes him ask and tells him he's being good when he does.

He uses traffic lights. He uses yellow more than he expected to, mostly when the praise lands too hard and he needs a second. He's working on not apologizing for that.

He'll crack jokes the whole time. It's how he handles anything nice. He'd like, someday, to need them a little less.
