On The Workbench

A spruce top with the braces glued and the go-bars still in. Twenty-four hours before I touch it. I could pull them early. It would look fine. It would fail in a year.

Next to it, a canvas roll with four clamps, a few clothespins, and brass weights I turned myself. Ten, twenty, fifty grams, stamped. I like knowing exactly what I'm asking someone to hold.

Pressure · Time · Release

__STATS_BAR__

A joint needs enough pressure to close the gap and not so much it starves. Then it needs to be left alone. I build guitars. I also do this.

My shop is a converted garage in West Asheville with a hundred and forty-two clamps on the wall. Those are for wood. They never touch you. What touches you comes out of a canvas roll: tweezer clamps, clovers, clothespins, weights. All adjustable. All tested on my own hand first.

I'll tell you how it goes, because surprises are for people who didn't plan. The first minute is sharp. Then it goes quiet. Coming off is the part you'll remember, when the blood comes back all at once. I'll tell you when it's coming and how long it lasts. I won't tell you what the timer says.

"I don't need you brave. I need you accurate."

Before anything, we sit at the bench with tea and write limits in pencil on the back of a build sheet. Traffic lights. I'll ask for a number from one to ten and I'll expect the real one. Red, and everything comes off, carefully, because stopping has a correct way to do it too.

I'm forty, I'm not in a hurry, and I'm told I'm hard to read. I'm looking for someone who can hold still under pressure and trust me to know when it's been long enough.
