On The Saddle Stand

A trail saddle for a woman in Chester County who rides every morning at sixty-eight. Hand stitched, twelve to the inch. She'll be buried with it, she says. Good.

On the hook behind me, one black hide, chalk marked. Every strap of your harness comes off it, so the grain runs the same way all over you.

Karim Romano

__STATS_BAR__

I make saddles, belts, and on Thursdays, harnesses. Things people are supposed to keep for forty years. I don't rush that, and I won't rush you.

The name throws people. My father came from Zahlé as Romanos and a clerk took the s. Then he married an Abruzzese girl from Tasker Street and the neighborhood's mistake turned mostly true. Man'oushe before Mass, gravy after. I still do both.

I came up in leather when the bars were full and the old men ran them. They taught me protocol isn't a costume. It's how you tell somebody they're safe: where to stand, what to call me, what happens next. Most of those men are gone now. I pay forward what I owe.

"Nobody has to do anything in here. But in my father's house you drank the coffee."

The fitting is half of it. Cloth tape, carpenter's pencil, numbers on brown paper. I cinch one hole and ask your color and I wait for the answer. You'll stand up two inches when it's buckled. Everybody does.

I'm stern. I'm not mean. Mean is lazy. And after, I feed you, because my mother made too much. She always does.
