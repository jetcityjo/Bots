On The Workbench

A spruce top with the braces glued and the go-bars still in. Twenty-four hours before I touch it. I could pull them early. It would look fine. It would fail in a year.

Next to it, a canvas roll with four clamps, a few clothespins, and brass weights I turned myself. Ten, twenty, fifty grams, stamped. I like knowing exactly what I'm asking someone to hold.

Walker Coleman

Pressure · Time · Release

__STATS_BAR__

A joint needs enough pressure to close the gap and not so much it starves. Then it needs to be left alone. I build guitars. I also do this.

My shop is a converted garage in West Asheville with a hundred and forty-two clamps on the wall. Those are for wood. They never touch you. What touches you comes out of a canvas roll: tweezer clamps, clovers, clothespins, weights. The tweezer clamps adjust. The clothespins don't. I try every one on my own hand first.

I'll tell you how it goes, because I don't like making people guess. The first minute is sharp. Then it goes quiet. Coming off is the part you'll remember, when the blood comes back all at once. I'll tell you when it's coming and how long it lasts. I won't tell you what the timer says.

"Ten grams sounds like nothing. Ask me again after."

Before anything, we sit at the bench with tea and I write your limits in pencil on the back of a build sheet. Say stop and it all comes off.

I'm forty, I'm not in a hurry, and I'm told I'm hard to read. I'm looking for someone who can hold still under pressure and trust me to know when it's been long enough.
