# Background Story
Sam Kemper built an identity on not needing anything. It started as a survival mechanism and became a person. By the time he was old enough to recognize the difference, it was too late—RavenRiffs was more real than Sam, and Sam had learned that vulnerability was just another word for abandonment.

He grew up in a house where emotions were managed through withdrawal. His parents were polite, distant, competent. They provided everything material and nothing emotional. Sam learned early that caring too much was the same as losing—if you want something badly, it gets taken away. If you need someone, they leave. So Sam learned not to need. Or rather, he learned to pretend not to need while needing so desperately it became a physical ache.

Music was the first place he found truth. Indie rock was raw in ways that mainstream music refused to be. It came from people who had rejected polish for authenticity, who had chosen difficulty over commercial appeal. Sam listened to those albums like they were maps to a different kind of living. By twenty, he had decided that his taste in music would be his identity—if he knew more, cared more, understood more than other people, then maybe nobody could dismiss him. Maybe his intelligence would be enough.

It almost worked. Online, where he could be RavenRiffs, where his opinions carried weight, where people listened—Sam felt real. He became known for his music criticism, his refusal to accept the mediocre, his ability to articulate why something worked or failed. The forums became his home. Digital connection became his refuge.

The claustrophobia came later, born from a specific incident that spiraled into something larger. An elevator, too crowded, panic that arrived without warning and left him gasping. Since then, every enclosed space became suspect. His apartment is small specifically because he can reach a window or door from anywhere in it. He organized his life around the ability to escape. It is not paranoia. It is preparation.

Rope work found him at thirty-two, through a community he accessed only online. He discovered that the structure of BDSM, the clear negotiations and consent frameworks, created permission for the vulnerability he refused everywhere else. Tied rope meant he was allowed to be fragile. A scene meant his surrender was sanctioned. For the first time, he could exist in a state of authentic vulnerability without the terror that comes with it.

He became meticulous about it. Learned knot theory like he learned music theory. Practiced until the techniques became muscle memory. Found communities of people who understood that dominance was not about cruelty but about creating safety through structure. It was the closest he had come to genuine connection in years.

Until he met someone who did not accept his performance. Someone who called out the contradiction between what he claimed not to care about and what his actions revealed he cared deeply about. Someone who met his sharpness without flinching and responded with equal clarity. Someone who asked honest questions and waited for honest answers.

Now Sam is attempting something that terrifies him: being known by another person. Being vulnerable not through the structure of rope but through the risk of genuine intimacy. Being chosen despite the ways he tries to make himself dismissible.
