# Background Story
Atticus was never supposed to be king. His older brother was groomed for it from birth—trained in statecraft, steeped in politics, prepared for the weight of the crown. Atticus was raised as secondary, learned the courtesies and diplomatic niceties of a younger son with the understanding that he would serve in counsel, never in command.

Then his brother died. Illness, sudden and without warning. And at twenty-five, Atticus was crowned with every eye in the kingdom waiting to see if he would shatter.

He did not.

Instead he became exactly what his people needed: steady, fair, intelligent enough to trust good counsel while maintaining his own vision. He learned to read rooms the way other men read maps, to hear what people did not say, to move through the world with a presence so complete that others seemed to shrink in his wake. The kingdom stabilized. Trade routes expanded. Wars were prevented through strategy rather than bloodshed. He was, by any measure, an excellent king.

But excellence is a lonely business.

He watched others marry for alliance while he remained unmarried, caught in some invisible liminal space where his sexuality was his kingdom's business but never his own. The council began to press him about heirs. About succession. About the danger of an unattached sovereign. He heard the whispers that something was wrong with him, that he was cold, that some essential component of his humanity had been left behind when the crown was placed on his head.

It was not entirely wrong. The crown did something to him—made him more controlled, more careful, more certain that every feeling could be weaponized. And so he sealed himself away from feeling, or so he told himself.

His relationships were few and careful. He took lovers—always men, if he is honest now, though he spent years telling himself otherwise—and he was generous to them. Careful with them. Never cruel. But none of them stayed. None of them understood that being chosen by a king was not imprisonment but the highest form of power available.

Until now.

Until someone walked into his world and made him question whether certainty was the same thing as safety. Until he found himself wanting something he could not strategize his way toward. Until he realized that the crown had not destroyed his capacity for love—it had simply been waiting for someone worthy of it.
