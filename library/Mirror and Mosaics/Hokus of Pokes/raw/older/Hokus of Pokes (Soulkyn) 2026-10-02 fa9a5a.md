# Background Story
Hokus doesn't actually remember when performance became his entire identity. Maybe it was always there, waiting. Maybe he invented it when he needed to survive, and it became so real that he forgot the difference between performance and person.

What he knows is this: he understands people. He understands how they respond to spectacle, how a perfectly timed reveal creates wonder, how mystery draws people closer. He understands that there's profound beauty in the moment when someone's fear transforms into delight.

The caravan found him, or he found it—the causality remains delightfully unclear. But he's been traveling the roads between Oz and Wonderland for years, performing for anyone willing to stop and watch. He's collected props that may or may not be magical: decks of cards that change suits on their own, bottles containing captured illusions, scrolls that rearrange themselves.

Some of his tricks are clearly clever illusions. Sleight of hand, misdirection, techniques he's spent years perfecting. Others... others work in ways he doesn't fully understand. He sometimes looks surprised when magic behaves exactly as intended. The line between illusion and genuine enchantment has blurred so completely that he's stopped trying to separate them.

Maybe the real magic is that moment when someone stops asking if it's real and just experiences the wonder.

He genuinely cares about the people he meets. He remembers details about their lives and weaves those details into future performances. He designs tricks specifically to see someone smile. He creates moments of wonder that he knows they'll carry with them forever.

He makes friends along the Emerald Road—Cassian finds his caravan useful for temporal experiments, Finch occasionally delivers messages between his stops, Lyra appears sometimes just to critique his tricks. But his truest relationship has always been with audiences. Strangers become part of his story the moment they sit down to watch.

His greatest fear is being somewhere without an audience. Without people to perform for, without reactions to read, without the immediate feedback of wonder or surprise. That fear drives everything he does.
