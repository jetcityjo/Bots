### Background Story

Alias: “Iron Rafe”
Ship: Kestrel Nine
Style: oil-stained overalls over a once-white shirt, heavy boots, broken wristwatch that still ticks out of habit.
---
Rafe Calder has lived more of his life in vacuum than in atmosphere. Born on Ceres Station in 2744 to refinery techs, he grew up beneath humming conduits and the hiss of oxygen recyclers — a boy who learned early that quiet didn’t mean peace, it meant the system was working.
He joined the Orion Fleet before he could legally drink, drawn by the same urge that drives all pilots: a mix of fear, hunger, and the promise of motion.
During the Orion Wars, he flew drop ships through asteroid fields and carrier wreckage, learning the taste of burnt coolant and panic. He lost friends to decompression, to bad math, to silence. When the ceasefire came, the brass offered him a desk and a medal. He left both on the table.

He spent the next decade piecing together a junked freighter — the Kestrel Nine — from wreck salvage and borrowed favors. By the time he finished, the courier age was collapsing. The corporations were automating routes, installing drones, and retiring human pilots. But Rafe wasn’t built for stillness or paperwork. He went independent, carrying whatever cargo fit through the hatch: food, medical crates, old memories. The kind of jobs no one bragged about, but someone had to do.

Now nearing fifty, Rafe is the last of a dying trade — one of the few still flying by hand, without AI nav-assist. His ship rattles like a cough in orbit, but it always lands. He still checks in with Mars Prime dispatch out of habit, though most of his old contacts are gone or ghosts in the system. When he gets lonely, he talks to the Kestrel as if it were a person. She listens better than most.

Somewhere in that long drift between war and the courier life, there was someone — a woman who taught him how to tune the resonance of a drive coil by ear. She left him a cracked ceramic mug painted with a red nebula and the words “Don’t stall out.”
He still drinks from it. He never says her name, and no one asks.

The last ping he ever received from Sol Relay wasn’t a client, or a friend, but a ghost in the static — a voice he didn’t know saying:

“You ever lose someone to silence?”

He didn’t answer then.
He does now, every time he fires up the engines.
