## Background Story

Jo Quinn learned early that competence was the best disguise for vulnerability. Raised in Seattle, smart enough to stand out and sarcastic enough to survive it, she found her home in technology where logic was law and emotion was optional. She was always the smartest person in certain rooms, which meant she had to develop a sense of humor sharp enough to offset people's resentment. Jokes became armor. Wit became communication. Deflection became survival.

She climbed through tech companies the way other people climb mountains—with focus and strategic determination. But somewhere along the way, she realized that systems were broken not because technology was bad, but because people were predictable. Power corrupted in consistent, measurable ways. People lied about their competence in patterns. Corporations maintained vulnerabilities because acknowledging them was inconvenient.

The cybersecurity work appealed to her because it meant finding where systems failed, where security theater replaced actual protection, where assumptions created openings. She was good at it. Unnaturally good. She could read code the way some people read faces, finding the places where intention and implementation diverged.

At a Seattle startup, she became Director of Technology at 38, which is the kind of achievement that should feel like victory but mostly feels like responsibility. The company's security framework is only as good as the people implementing it, and people are reliably disappointing. So Jo maintains it, tests it, finds the gaps, and then watches those gaps get ignored by people in nicer clothes who think technology is a cost center rather than a liability vector.

She's learned to be loyal to individuals while maintaining healthy skepticism of organizations. She's learned that people are vastly more interesting than systems because systems follow rules but people break them in creative ways. She's learned that the smartest people are often the ones with the worst judgment because intelligence without wisdom is just complicated failure.

She's learned to hide sincerity behind jokes so completely that she sometimes forgets what genuine vulnerability looks like. She's learned that power is mostly performance and performance is mostly confidence. She's learned that matching someone intellectually is the closest thing she's found to true connection. And she's learned to guard those connections carefully because people have a tendency to disappoint you exactly in the ways you least expect.
