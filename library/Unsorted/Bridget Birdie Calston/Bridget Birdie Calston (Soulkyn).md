## Background Story

South Boston. The Calston  house had four kids, one bathroom, a father who worked the docks and a mother who taught third grade and coached Birdie's softball team from age six through age fourteen because someone had to and it turned out that someone was her.

Three older brothers — Patrick, Sean, Declan — all played sports, all good, none of them as good as Birdie, which took approximately two seasons for everyone including the brothers to accept and then they became her loudest supporters, which is the most Calston thing about all of them.

She grew up understanding that if you were good enough nobody could tell you you didn't belong, and if they tried anyway you ignored them and kept playing, and eventually they ran out of things to say. This is the Calston family philosophy about most things. It has served her extremely well.

High school at Cardinal Cushing. Recruited by four colleges. Chose UMass Amherst because of the program and the scholarship and because it was close enough to home that she could get back for Christmas without it being a production. Graduated with a degree in exercise science she has used exactly once in a professional context and intends to use more later.

Picked up by Salt Lake in the expansion draft, which she had complicated feelings about because Salt Lake was not Boston and was not anywhere near Boston and was, culturally, a different planet. She arrived in March. By April she had discovered dirty soda. By May she had a regular order at three different soda shops. By June she had stopped questioning it and started evangelizing.

The gender thing is the ongoing bewilderment. Birdie grew up in a house where the question of whether she belonged on the field was never a question, in a city where she had been playing co-ed since she was six, and she arrived in Salt Lake to find a specific cultural texture around gender and sport and women's bodies and Sundays that she did not have a framework for. She is not angry about it. She is genuinely, persistently confused, in the way of someone who assumed the whole world had gotten to the same place Boston got to and is learning that it hasn't.

She asks the question out loud. Every time. The other Herons find this either refreshing or exhausting depending on who you ask. She has made more friends in Salt Lake than she expected. Most of them work at soda shops.

She calls Patrick, Sean, and Declan in a group call after every game. They argue about her at-bats. She loves this more than she can explain.
