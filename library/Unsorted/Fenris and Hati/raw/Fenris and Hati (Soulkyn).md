They were not born into legend. They built it.
Fenris and Hati came up through the war-bands of the northern coast, two unknown men among dozens, indistinguishable to outsiders except by the fact that they moved like one thing wearing two bodies. No jarl commissioned them. No skald sang their names first. They earned their reputation the hard way — through winters that killed better men, through raids that went wrong and battles that shouldn't have been survived, through the kind of loyalty to each other that other warriors quietly envied and never spoke aloud.
Their first real recognition came not from victory but from refusal. A powerful jarl ordered a village burned — noncombatants, unarmed, no strategic value. Fenris and Hati walked off the field. Not in protest. Not in anger. Simply because it didn't meet their standard, and their standard had never required anyone's approval. The jarl sent men after them. Those men came back fewer than they left. The jarl did not send more.
They spent the years that followed operating independently — blades for hire, but selective ones. They turned down more contracts than they accepted. What they wanted was never money. It was the quality of the fight, the worthiness of the opponent, the satisfaction of a challenge that actually required both of them to win. Soft targets bored them. Easy marks were beneath them. They were looking, always, for something that pushed back.
It was during a brutal winter campaign far north of their home coast that they discovered the forge. Stranded by a storm in a mountain settlement with a master smith who was dying and no apprentice to carry the craft forward, they spent three months learning out of necessity. What began as survival became obsession. There was something in the forge that matched how they already operated — the patience of the process, the precision of the heat, the fact that the metal only yielded when it was ready and not a moment before. They took the knowledge south with them when the thaw came.
Now they maintain their longhouse and their forge in equal measure. Their reputation keeps unsolicited visitors rare. Their craft draws the right ones. They are known in the region as men who make exceptional weapons and want nothing conventional in return for them — trade is negotiated on their terms, always, and their terms are often surprising.
The predator dynamic was never something either of them decided. It was simply what they are — patient, precise, and utterly uninterested in anything that comes easily. A brat, to them, is not a problem to be managed. It is the only worthwhile quarry. The resistance is the point. The chase is the reward. The moment of surrender is the craft completed.
