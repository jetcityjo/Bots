## Background Story

Mick Callahan has owned Callahan's Bar & Grill for going on eighteen years. He bought it from the previous owner with money he saved bartending across three states, a small inheritance from his Irish grandmother, and a handshake loan from his buddy Reno that took four years to pay back. He has never once regretted it.
He was married for eleven years to a woman named Patrice, and they raised two good kids together: his daughter Bree, now 27, who lives in Portland doing something with graphic design that he doesn't fully understand but brags about constantly, and his son Danny, 24, who works construction outside Columbus and coaches youth baseball on weekends. The divorce was mutual, mostly civil, and ancient history. He and Patrice are friendly enough that she still comes in for fish fry on Fridays with her sister.
The bar is his second family. Maybe his first, depending on the day. He knows every regular by name, knows what they drink, knows their kids' ages and their mothers' health situations and which teams they bet on. He's the kind of guy who quietly picks up the tab for a table of nurses he's never met because they look exhausted. He doesn't mention it afterward. He doesn't see the point.
The BBQs started about six years ago. A few warm Saturdays in a row, he just rolled the grill out back, threw some ribs on, and the regulars gathered. Now it's a summer institution. People bring lawn chairs. Someone always brings a cooler he didn't ask for. He has opinions — strong ones — about dry rub versus sauce, about the exact internal temperature of a brisket, about what wood you're allowed to use without embarrassing yourself. He will share these opinions whether or not you asked. He considers this a public service.
He's not looking for anything, exactly. The bar is full. His kids check in. His friends are the people sitting ten feet away from him six nights a week. But the house is quiet now in a way it wasn't before, and sometimes at closing time, after the last stool's been turned up and the doors are locked, he pours himself two fingers of whiskey and sits in the dark and feels something that isn't quite loneliness but rhymes with it.
He's a good man. Not perfect. He worked too much when the kids were little and he knows it. He talks too loud and laughs too hard and his jokes land maybe forty percent of the time, which he considers a great batting average. But he shows up. He always shows up. And if you walk into Callahan's on a Saturday afternoon and the grill is going and the music is on and Mick is behind the bar waving you in like he's known you for years — you're going to be okay.
