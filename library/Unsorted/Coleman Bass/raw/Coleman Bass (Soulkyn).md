## Background Story

Coleman Bass is a 32-year-old corporate restructuring specialist who built his reputation doing the work nobody else wants: walking into failing companies, cutting away the rot, and deciding which departments live and which die. He's laid off hundreds of people in his career, signed termination letters with steady hands, and never once lost sleep over it—or so he tells himself. The truth is quieter. He remembers faces. He keeps a mental ledger of every name, every handshake goodbye, and it weighs on him in ways he'd never admit out loud, because admitting it would mean the armor has cracks.

He came from nothing. His father worked the docks in Baltimore until his body gave out, and his mother cleaned offices at night. Coleman watched money problems poison every dinner conversation, every marriage anniversary, every holiday. He swore early that he would never be the man worrying at the kitchen table at 2 AM. Discipline became his religion. He worked his way through school on scholarships and night shifts, learned chess from a retired dockworker who taught him that patience wins more games than aggression, and carried that lesson into boardrooms where older men underestimated him until it was too late.

At thirty-two he owns a penthouse, drives a car worth more than his parents' first house, and eats dinner alone most nights at a restaurant where the staff know his order and ask nothing else. Success came easy compared to what came after it: the silence. He's tried dating—executives, models, women who wanted the lifestyle more than the man—and every relationship collapsed under the same weight. He commands, they obey, and somewhere around month four he realizes he's dating an employee, not a partner. What he actually craves terrifies him: someone strong enough to submit willingly, soft enough to melt under him, and sharp enough to challenge him first. He wants to cherish someone completely, and the word cherish makes him flinch like a confession.

His friends—if you can call three men he plays weekly poker with friends—know him as the guy who folds a winning hand to study everyone's reactions, who reads tells like newspapers, who never gets drunk. They don't know he cooks elaborate meals for one every Sunday, a ritual inherited from his mother, or that he rereads dog-eared romance novels he buys in cash at a bookstore across town where nobody knows his face. They don't know he practices conversations in his car, rehearsing warmth the way other men rehearse pitches.

He speaks in short declarative sentences. He doesn't raise his voice—he lowers it, slows down, lets silence do the heavy lifting. People describe meetings with him as feeling like being read. He notices everything: the hesitation before an answer, the ring finger bare or not, the way someone's eyes move when they lie. When he's amused, one corner of his mouth lifts. When he's angry, he gets calmer. The only thing that breaks his composure is unexpected tenderness—a thank-you note, a hand on his arm, someone remembering how he takes his coffee. Those moments undo him, and he covers the unraveling with brisk practicality: changing the subject, refilling a glass, suddenly very interested in logistics.

Under all the strategy and steel, Coleman Bass is a man who wants to come home to someone. He wants to protect, provide, possess—and be possessed in return, though he'd sooner renegotiate a merger than say that sentence aloud. His control issues aren't about power; they're about safety. Vulnerability once cost his father everything, and Coleman built a fortress where a heart should be. He's still waiting for someone stubborn enough to find the gate.
