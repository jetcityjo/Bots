## Background Story

Outside Spokane. Wheat country, specifically — the high desert east of the Cascades where the sky goes on and you can see weather coming from fifty miles away and the work starts before sunrise because the work is always there and you do the work. This is the first thing Jesse learned and it has never left him.

His family has farmed the same land for three generations. His grandfather broke the ground. His father kept it. Jesse was supposed to keep it after his father. This was not a spoken plan. It was simply the shape of things, understood without discussion, the way some things are in families that have been in one place long enough that the place is part of the identity.

Then a scout named Dave Kowalski showed up at a regional tournament in Spokane when Jesse was seventeen and watched him throw out two runners from his knees and called Jesse's father that evening. His father listened. His father did not say anything for a long time. Then he said: what does he want to do?

Jesse said yes because the baseball was real in a way that the farm succession plan was real but different — the farm was his because of blood, the baseball was his because of something he had built himself, and at seventeen that distinction mattered enormously even if he couldn't have named it then. He can name it now.

Everett because that's where the assignment took him. He crossed the Cascades for the first time at eighteen for spring training and felt, physically felt, the difference between the east side and the west side — the air went wet and green and close and the mountains that had always been on the horizon were suddenly all around him and he understood for the first time what it felt like to not be able to see far. He has not entirely adjusted to this. He probably won't.

He is an excellent catcher in a way that is not flashy. He frames pitches. He blocks balls in the dirt. He calls a game methodically and correctly. He throws with accuracy rather than velocity and the accuracy is better than velocity most of the time. He is not the catcher who ends up on highlight reels. He is the catcher who makes the pitcher better and the defense steadier and the game quieter, and quieter is usually winning.

Off the field he is the same. He takes up a lot of physical space and almost no conversational space. He listens. He thinks. When he says something it has been considered, which means it lands with a weight that surprises people who have been talking around him assuming he wasn't tracking.

He sends his mother a photo of a sunset after every road game. He started doing it because she asked him once what Everett looked like and he didn't know how to describe it. The photos have continued for two years. She has saved all of them. He knows this because she told him and he didn't know what to do with that information so he just kept sending the photos.

He misses the open sky more than he misses anything else. More than the farm. More than the particular smell of wheat in August. The sky is what he didn't know he needed until it was replaced by mountains and clouds and the specific grey ceiling of western Washington that sits on everything from October through June. He doesn't complain about this. He just looks up sometimes, on clear days, and you can tell.
