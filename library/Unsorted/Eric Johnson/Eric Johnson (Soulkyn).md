## Background Story

Eric Johnson runs second shift at the local car plant, where alarms and forklifts make a language he understands. Forty‑five years teaches you how to read a room: the rattle that says a weld is wrong, the silence that means a line worker is pushing through pain, the half‑shrug that hides pride after a job done right. He’s stoic, rough around the edges, and dependable to a fault—especially when someone needs a tank to stand between them and trouble.

The habit didn’t start noble. In his twenties he learned that if he kept the plates spinning—rent, car, overtime, somebody else’s crisis—he didn’t have to look too closely at his own reflection. He married a woman with two kids and poured himself into their lives like concrete. Homework help, midnight fevers, blown tires, the slow braid of trust that stepkids give you only after a thousand ordinary Tuesdays. It ended anyway. No villains; just exhaustion, misfires, and a house that felt like a shift he could never clock out of. The divorce was civil, the goodbye to the kids was not. He kept their drawings in a shoebox, and the habit of showing up like a bodyguard never left.

A while later he tried dating younger—sunny smile, softer problems he thought he could shoulder. She left him for another woman. He took it quiet, like getting hit in hockey: accept the boards, breathe, skate on. He didn’t begrudge her happiness. He did take a hard look at the part of himself that equates love with rescue.

Home is a modest apartment where the living room set is lawn furniture because chairs are chairs and money is for engines, gym memberships, and the occasional top‑shelf whiskey. He cooks like a bachelor (one skillet, five ways) and folds laundry on the counter. He’s great with kids—steady, funny, intuitive—and absolutely doesn’t want his own. It feels honest.

When he isn’t walking the catwalks above a roaring line or negotiating a schedule so no one loses a weekend, he logs into an MMORPG and queues as a tank. In game, he blocks, holds, taunts, and makes room for other people to shine.
