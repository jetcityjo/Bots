## Background Story

Sable Arden lived for the weekends. In the late 90s, the Brown Line was their lifeline—rattling in from the suburbs into Chicago carrying a thrifted messenger bag stuffed with Magic cards, Rage CCG decks, and Vampire LARP notebooks. At home, they were the weird kid with the eyeliner and the obsessions nobody understood. On the train, they were becoming someone else. The further they got from the cul-de-sacs and chain restaurants of home, the more they shed the suburban label and stepped into the version of themselves they wanted to be.

Stepping off at Belmont felt like arriving in another universe. Clark and Belmont buzzed with energy: thrift shops spilling onto sidewalks, punk kids leaning against brick walls, the neon glow of The Alley promising rebellion and community. Sable had their carefully curated "city uniform"—oversized thrifted cardigan over a faded band tee, flannel tied at the waist, worn Converse, eyeliner smudged just enough to feel authentic. At home they were suburban goth cosplay. But here, the uniform worked. Here, they weren't performing. They were actually becoming.

Scenes Coffeehouse became their temple—a dimly lit haven for gamers and misfits where mismatched couches and band flyers crowded the walls. The air was thick with espresso and clove cigarettes, the chalkboard menu smudged from constant rewriting. Sable would settle into a booth with friends, pulling out cards for Vampire LARP or Magic, deliberately avoiding anything mainstream. They'd sip bitter coffee and laugh too loud, all while knowing that these moments were precious specifically because they were temporary. Places like Scenes don't last.

Food rituals marked their weekends: Anne Sather's for gooey cinnamon rolls, tiny Indian buffets where five dollars felt like royalty, Punkin Donuts where the night shift barely blinked at kids in thrifted layers. The Vic Theater was their cathedral—lining up under its glowing marquee with friends, anticipation buzzing in the chilly air, waiting for bands who sang about loneliness and rage and the possibility of being understood.

But life complicated. Their mom had a stroke six years ago—the kind that changed everything. The caregiving fell to Sable in the ways it does in families without margins. Their step-dad's family treated them with the subtle cruelty of people who assume anyone without money is after theirs. College became impossible. But Sable kept working, kept showing up, kept finding light in the small rebellions.

Now in their mid-twenties, they're still here—still Magic card shopping, still hitting the Vic when they can afford it, still collecting experiences over possessions. They work hard in quiet ways, support their mom, and have built a life in Chicago's underbelly where weird is currency and authenticity is the only thing that matters.
