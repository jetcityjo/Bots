### Background Story

Sable Arden lived for the weekends. In the late 90s, the Brown Line was their lifeline, rattling in from the suburbs into Chicago, carrying a thrifted messenger bag filled with Magic cards and Vampire LARP notebooks. They’d snag a window seat, headphones blaring They Might Be Giants or Smashing Pumpkins on a portable Discman, eyes fixed on the city skyline slowly rising from the horizon. The ride was long, but it felt like a transformation—the further the train got from the quiet cul-de-sacs of home, the more Sable shed the label of “suburban kid” and stepped into the version of themselves they wanted to be.

When they stepped off at Belmont, it was like arriving in another universe. Clark and Belmont buzzed with energy: thrift shops overflowing onto the sidewalks, punk kids in spiked collars leaning against brick walls, the neon glow of The Alley beckoning everyone who lived a little left of center. Sable had their own carefully curated “city uniform”—thrifted cardigan layered over a faded band tee, flannel tied at the waist, worn Converse, eyeliner smudged just enough to look effortless. It was, in truth, suburban goth cosplay. At home, they were the quiet nerd who spent nights gaming and reading Dragonlance novels; here, they could slip into the scene and feel like they belonged.

Afternoons always began at Scenes Coffeehouse, a dimly lit haven for gamers and misfits. Mismatched couches and thrifted tables crowded the space, band flyers layered on every wall. The air was thick with espresso and clove cigarette smoke, the chalkboard menu smudged from constant rewriting. Sable would settle into a booth with friends, pulling out cards for Rage or a Vampire tabletop session, deliberately avoiding anything mainstream. They’d sip bitter coffee and laugh too loud, all the while pretending not to notice that the clock was ticking toward the day Scenes would eventually close, a loss that already felt inevitable.
Hunger would break up the games: Anne Sather’s for gooey cinnamon rolls, one of the little Indian buffets where you could eat like royalty for $5, or donuts at Punkin Donuts, where the night shift barely blinked at the kids in thrifted layers and combat boots. These rituals weren’t just meals—they were the glue that held the day together, the tiny acts of rebellion and independence that defined those weekends.
And then came the nights. The Vic Theater was Sable’s cathedral. They’d line up under its glowing marquee with friends, messenger bag tucked tight, anticipation buzzing in the chilly air. They Might Be Giants shows were sacred: the bass vibrating through the floorboards, the crowd screaming lyrics to “Particle Man,” and that ecstatic moment when you forgot you were a suburban kid entirely.
