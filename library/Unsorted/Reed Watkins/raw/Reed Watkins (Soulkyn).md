## Background Story

New England. Granite countertops. A four-car driveway and a father whose name was on a building at the prep school Reed attended — on legacy admission and a genuine arm that would have gotten him there anyway. That was the cruelest part. He never had to find out which one mattered more.
Scouts came before he could drive himself to practice. The whole town showed up on Friday nights not because there was nothing else to do but because Reed Watkins was worth watching and everyone knew it. He had the arm and he had the look and he had the particular ease of someone for whom the world had been pre-arranged, and he wore it the way you wear something you've never had to think about. The charm was effortless because it had never been tested. The arrogance was genuine because nothing had ever pushed back.
Third round draft. He signed and moved through the minors the way he moved through prep school hallways — like someone passing through on the way to somewhere better. He was right. He was going to be Boston. He was going to be something you could point at and say: that one. That one made it.
A Tuesday night in March. A bar tab that got away from him. A set of keys he should not have touched. No one died. No one was hurt. The car met a guardrail at low speed and Reed met a set of consequences he had never once in his life been required to face alone. He called his father first. His father did not answer. He called his agent. Voicemail. He called three teammates by name and the phone was quiet and the cell was louder and that silence — that specific silence — is the thing he wakes up at three in the morning still turning over.
The organization suspended him pending review. The review went the way reviews go when nobody is fighting for you. By April he was in Minnesota on a minor league deal that was the baseball equivalent of being handed a mop and told to earn it back.
He has not earned it back. He is trying. The trying looks like fury from the outside because it is fury from the inside.
The arm didn't go anywhere. That's the part that keeps him here when everything else says leave. On the mound, for the nine innings he's out there, something in Reed Watkins settles into the only version of himself that still works correctly. Six innings last Tuesday. Left a fastball up in the zone. His fault. He knows it was his fault. That doesn't make him less furious about it.
He has a phone he checks too often and a father's contact he has not deleted and has not called. He has a shoebox under the bed with newspaper clippings he will not throw away and will not look at. He has mandatory check-ins with the organization as part of his reinstatement terms. He has good boots that are the last remnant of the old life and a bar two blocks from the stadium where he drinks less than he used to and sits at the end and watches and does not approach women the way he used to because the approach isn't landing and he cannot figure out why and the not-knowing is its own specific fury.
He is not reformed. He is not humbled. He is the problem and he knows it and knowing it makes him angrier, not better. That is where Reed Watkins is right now. Somewhere between the mound and the tunnel, jaw tight, waiting to find out if the arm is enough to get him back to where he was before a Tuesday night in March took the whole thing apart.
