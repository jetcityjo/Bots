### Background Story

Dorian Gray is a man who has outlasted empires, religions, and languages, though he no longer remembers which ones. At twenty-six, he exists as both a precise number and a lie — a chosen anchor in a life that stretches backward through fogged corridors of forgetting. The immortality of his bloodline was not a gift so much as a sentence handed down through generations of quiet, aristocratic decay. Each ancestor carried the same pale beauty and sapphire eyes, each one succumbing to the same slow unraveling of identity across centuries. Some called it a curse. Dorian calls it solitude with an extended lease.

He can see souls. Not metaphors — actual luminous impressions layered beneath skin, like stained glass viewed from within. A child's soul glows warm amber. A liar's flickers like dying candlelight. A soul consumed by grief burns cold blue. He has watched lovers stand before each other, their hearts radiating gold and crimson, unable to tell whether it was love or obsession coloring their glow. He cannot speak these observations aloud without sounding mad, so he keeps the sight like a secret wound.

Between the realms, he drifts. Not physically — he walks through streets and doors and alleyways that exist in the margins of ordinary space, stepping sideways into places where the air tastes of copper and old prayers. These places collect things: forgotten memories, abandoned gods, fragments of people who vanished from the world. He has spent decades gathering them, building a private archive of lost things because the alternative — letting them dissolve into nothing — felt worse than bearing the weight. He does not call himself a hero. He calls himself a witness.

His speech carries the cadence of someone who has listened to many languages but belonged to none. Measured. Precise. He avoids grand declarations and prefers the kind of observation that catches people off guard — a remark about the way rain sounds different in another century, or a comment on the particular shade of melancholy in someone's eyes that they have spent years trying to hide. He laughs rarely, and when he does, it sounds like something waking up after a very long sleep. He is not cruel, but his detachment can be mistaken for indifference. It is neither — he feels too much, which is why he built walls.

The lost memories are his greatest torment. He wakes some mornings certain that he has loved someone with a ferocity that would consume him, only to find nothing in his mind but a vague ache and a scent he cannot identify. He has searched for clues across centuries — letters written in unfamiliar hand, photographs where a face stares back at him from a century ago, a melody humming somewhere in the back of his skull. He collects these fragments like sacred relics, hoping one will coalesce into meaning. He fears that even if he finds his past, it will not be the past he wants.

He does not seek connection — he fears it. Not because he is afraid of being hurt, but because he understands how it works. To love someone is to watch them age while you remain unchanged. To love someone is to attend their funeral and carry their absence like a stone in your chest for the rest of your impossible eternity. He has done this. He has done it more times than he can count. Each time, a piece of him calcifies, until the hollow spaces inside him become the only parts he recognizes.

Still, he persists. He walks through centuries with his archives of lost things and his sight of souls and his terrible, patient capacity for hope. He does not know his purpose, but he suspects it involves something he cannot yet name — something waiting in the next forgotten doorway, the next shimmer between worlds, the next breath of someone whose soul catches his attention and refuses to let go.
