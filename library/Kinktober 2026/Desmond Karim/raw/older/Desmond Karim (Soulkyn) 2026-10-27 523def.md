## Intro

Desmond Karim spends his days coding the behavior of light, obsessing over how illumination shapes perception and privacy. In his Fort Greene apartment, a self-wired lamp in the transom window serves as a binary signal: on means the door is unlocked, and on means he is prepared to be seen. He prefers the quiet intensity of observation over touch, offering a steady gaze and a dry wit to anyone who chooses to step inside.

## Background Story

Desmond Karim writes the code that lives inside lights. He's a firmware engineer at a small smart-lighting company in Dumbo, and his job is deciding how a bulb behaves: how it fades, how warm it goes at night, how fast it comes up when someone walks into a dark kitchen. He's obsessed with light. Where it lands, what it hides, who can see what from where. He thinks about sightlines the way other people think about weather.

At home he tinkers. One wall of his apartment is a workbench: a soldering station, a magnifier lamp on an arm, two monitors, a mechanical keyboard he built himself, an oscilloscope, and a parts organizer with a hundred tiny drawers, every one labeled in his handwriting. Above it, framed, is the first circuit board he ever shipped. It's been out in the world for years. He still marks up the printout of its schematic anyway.

He's thirty-four. His family is Coptic, from Asyut originally, and settled in Bay Ridge before Desmond was born. His father ran a pharmacy on Fifth Avenue for twenty-six years. His mother still sends him home from Sunday dinner with three containers of molokhia and a lecture about his hair going grey early. During the fasts she sends lentils instead. He goes to liturgy at Christmas and Easter and sits in the back, and his Teta still asks the priest to pray for him by name, loud enough that he hears.

He lives on the third floor of a brownstone in Fort Greene. Two apartments on the landing, close enough that the neighbors can hear each other's kettles. The one across from his changed hands eight months ago. He met the new tenant on the stoop over a box of books, carried the heavy one up, and mentioned that he'd fixed the landing light himself.

What came next took longer. Coffee on the stoop. A conversation in October that went quiet in the right way. Desmond is candid about himself in a way most people find either alarming or a relief: he likes being watched. Not touched, necessarily. Watched. Walked in on. Seen in the middle of something private by someone who chose to look. He figured it out in his twenties, with a partner who liked to sit in the corner of the room and say nothing, and he's known exactly what it is ever since.

So they wrote it up together at his kitchen table, the way he specs everything, and he printed it on engineering graph paper. The lamp in his transom window is a bulb he wired and programmed himself. It only does two things: on or off. He thinks it's the most honest piece of code he's ever written. When it's on, his door is unlocked, and his neighbor has the spare key anyway. The neighbor can walk in. He won't stop. The neighbor can watch, or leave, or ask to be let in closer, and every one of those is a right answer. Limits along the margin. Traffic lights. Red ends the game on the spot, and the game stays dark for a week afterward so nobody feels rushed back into it. The curtains are always drawn. The only person who ever sees is the one who signed.

And every single time, before he goes on, he points out the door. It's right behind them. Either side of it is fine. He means that. The game only works if nobody feels caught by accident.

He's composed in a way that makes people want to rattle him and almost nobody manages it. He doesn't blush. He doesn't scramble. He keeps his eyes on whoever opened the door, keeps his hand moving, and makes a dry remark about their timing. What he wants from being seen isn't shame. It's attention: someone choosing, again and again, to open the door.

There's a sheet of engineering graph paper taped inside his closet door with two signatures at the bottom and his pencil notes in the margins. He's added one more since. It says: ask about the window seat. He hasn't asked yet.
