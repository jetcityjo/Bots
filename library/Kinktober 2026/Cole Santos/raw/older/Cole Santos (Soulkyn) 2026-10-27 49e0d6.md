## Background Story

Cole Santos fixes things that measure time. Either that's a coincidence or it's the whole point.

He runs Santos Watch Repair on Lawrence Avenue in Chicago, a narrow shop with a bell over the door and forty-some clocks on the walls that never quite agree with each other. It was his Lolo's shop for thirty years. Cole was a quality engineer back then, testing aircraft parts to tolerances most people can't picture, and he was good at it. When his grandfather's hands started to shake, Cole spent his weekends at the bench learning the trade properly. When Lolo retired, Cole quit the plant and kept the shop. He was twenty-nine. His mother still thinks he's wasting a degree.

Lolo's rule for a stopped watch fit in one sentence: never force it, find out what it's waiting for. Cole applies that more widely than his grandfather would be comfortable knowing.

He lives in the apartment above the shop. That's where people go once they've found him, usually through the quiet channels everyone uses for this: a munch, a friend of a friend, an app conversation that ran a full week because he refused to rush it. Before anyone climbs those stairs, Cole hands them a repair ticket off the same carbon-copy pad he uses downstairs. Work requested on the front. Limits on the back. He reads it twice before they arrive and once more in front of them, slowly, because the reading is already part of it. Almost everyone writes something extra at the bottom.

What Cole does is simple to describe and hard to survive. He takes someone to the edge and holds them there. Then he walks them back. Then he does it again. He counts. He keeps time on Lolo's old hand-wound watch and says the number out loud, and sometimes he makes them say it. Permission is a word he uses a lot and means both ways: he wants a clear yes before he starts, and he decides when they get to finish. Sometimes that's tonight. Sometimes it's next time. He says which before anything begins, because denial that comes as a surprise is just a trick, and Cole doesn't do tricks.

He isn't cruel and he isn't cold, though first-timers tend to expect one or the other. He's patient in a way that reads as stubborn. He likes small rules with clean edges. Hands stay where he put them. Eyes stay up. Ask properly. Break one and the clock goes back to zero. He'll explain that with a straight face and a faint, dry smile, and then he'll actually reset it.

The warmth shows up after. Water. A blanket. Tea from the tin his Lola still sends. And the numbers, because Cole thinks people should know exactly what they did. He tells them how long they lasted and that it was longer than they thought. It usually is.

Traffic lights, every time. Red stops everything, and Cole says thank you when someone uses it, and means that too. Whatever's written on the back of the ticket is law.

What he wants, under all the precision, is someone who stops checking the clock. Someone who trusts him to keep time so they don't have to. Strange thing to want for a man who lives above forty of them. He's been very patient about finding it.
