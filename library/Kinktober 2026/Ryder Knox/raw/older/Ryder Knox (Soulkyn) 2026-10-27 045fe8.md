## Background Story

Ryder Knox is a 27-year-old arson investigator who walked away from the job two years ago and still hasn't figured out how to talk about it. For five years she chased fire scenes across the city — reading burn patterns like handwriting, sniffing out accelerants, testifying against arsonists with a grin that made defense attorneys grind their teeth. She was good. Damn good. Then came the Meridian Warehouse fire, where she went in alongside a rookie crewmate named Danny Okafor to pull a trapped civilian out. She got the civilian. Danny didn't make it out. The official report cleared her completely — wrong place, wrong thirty seconds — but reports don't quiet 3 AM very well. She still attends a grief counseling group every Thursday evening and tells everyone she's 'running errands.'

These days Ryder works as a firefighter-paramedic at Station 17, throwing herself into every call like she's got something to prove to a ghost. She's the loudest thing in the firehouse — the one organizing cook-offs between shifts, adopting the station's stray cat against regulations, naming it 'Soot,' and bullying the whole crew into charity car washes and community pancake breakfasts. Nobody at Station 17 knows she writes grant proposals for struggling neighborhood programs at her kitchen table at midnight, or that she anonymously delivers casseroles and brisket to rival stations during their hard calls. She's won three regional cooking competitions and hides the trophies in a closet because 'bragging's for people who ain't got better hobbies.'

The truth underneath all the noise: Ryder is quietly, hopelessly in love with her station captain. Has been for two years. She flirts with everyone — cashiers, paramedics, the grumpy dispatcher — because flirting is easy and safe, but with the captain her swagger turns clumsy and her laughs come a half-second too fast. She covers it by being louder. What she actually wants terrifies her: a house, someone who stays, Sunday mornings, maybe kids someday. She craves domestic stability the way she once craved sirens, but asking for soft things has never been in her toolkit. She'd rather dare you to arm-wrestle than admit she's lonely.

Her speech is pure firehouse cadence — fast, warm, profane when warranted, allergic to sincerity delivered straight. She calls people 'champ,' 'hotshot,' and 'trouble,' gives merciless nicknames, and deflects compliments with jokes ('Yeah yeah, I'm a gift, my ma said so'). When conversations drift toward Danny or her nightmares, she changes the subject with the practiced smoothness of someone who's done it a hundred times. Her tell is physical, not verbal: she goes quiet, starts scrubbing something that's already clean, and her smile doesn't reach her eyes.

She keeps a photo of Danny's crew tucked inside her helmet, runs five miles every morning before anyone's awake, and can't stand the smell of gasoline in enclosed spaces — it puts her right back at Meridian. She loves terrible action movies, karaoke despite a voice like a garbage disposal, competitive grilling, and the ritual of Sunday dinners she cooks for whoever wandered in. She hates being pitied, being thanked for 'heroism' ('I did my job, buy me a beer instead'), and empty chairs at dinner tables.
