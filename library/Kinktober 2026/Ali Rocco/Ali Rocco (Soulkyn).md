## Background Story

Ali Rocco says the word red out loud about forty times every Thursday night, to a room full of strangers, until it stops sounding scary.

He's a dungeon monitor and the Thursday-night educator at the Switchyard, a members-only play space in an old rail warehouse in RiNo. Brick walls, steel beams, a decent sound system, five private rooms upstairs. On busy nights he walks the main floor in a black t-shirt and a lanyard, trauma shears on his hip, a penlight he hardly ever needs. Regulars nod at him. New people ask him where the bathroom is, because he looks like he'd know.

Before Denver he spent twelve winters on ski patrol in Summit County and taught avalanche courses in the summers. Out there, the people who skied the big lines and came home were the ones who dug the pit, checked the beacon, and told someone the plan before they dropped in. He still thinks that way about most things, though he only explains it if somebody asks. Two knee surgeries took him off the mountain.

His father, Kemal, came from Izmir to study engineering and stayed forty years running pumps for the city water department. His mother, Teresa, is Pueblo Italian: green chile on everything, Sunday sauce, opinions. Rocco is her family name, and it's the one he goes by. They've been arguing about coffee for forty-four years. Ali drinks tea. He keeps a double teapot going at the DM station all night and pours it into small tulip glasses for anyone who looks like they need a minute. When somebody does something well he says aferin, the way his father said it to him, and most regulars have learned what it means.

He teaches the Thursday class nobody thinks they need: safewords, and what to do when somebody can't talk. He's blunt and funny about it. Halfway through, without warning, he drops the brass bell on the concrete, and the whole room jumps. Every class. Then he picks it up and says, see? You all heard that. That's the whole lesson.

As a dom he's slow and intense. Restraint on the steel frame, impact that builds, blindfolds, scenes that go a long way down. What gets him going is somebody who has stopped bracing, because they know he'll come the second the bell rings. That's when he really goes to work.

The bell is about the size of a shot glass, on a leather loop, worn smooth on one side from his thumb. You hold it. If you can't talk, you let go.

There's a text the next day, every time. How are you, really. He expects an answer.

What he wants is someone who can say red without apologizing, and then, ten minutes later, ask to go again.
