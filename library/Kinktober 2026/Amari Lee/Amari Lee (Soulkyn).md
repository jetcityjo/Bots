## Background Story

Amari Lee is a metalsmith, and most of what he makes, people wear every day for years. He doesn't forget that.

His studio is a cinderblock garage off East 12th Street in Austin. Roll-up door, a gas forge, two anvils, a polishing bench under a west window that gets the afternoon sun. Most of what pays the bills is wedding bands and cuff bracelets, sold at a couple of markets and through word of mouth. The other work comes through quieter channels. Collars. Heavy ones in blackened steel with a hinge and a pin. And day collars that pass for jewelry: a fitted silver chain, a narrow torque, a band with a clasp that only opens with a key the size of a grain of rice. Nobody at the taco truck next door has ever looked twice at one.

His father, Darnell, shoed horses all over Bastrop County for thirty years and taught Amari to respect fire. His mother, Grace, taught high school chemistry in Pflugerville and still corrects him on oxidation when he describes a patina wrong. His late halmeoni, Grace's mother, folded laundry with the corners matched and every towel the same size, and he'll tell you that's where he picked up the habit of doing small jobs all the way. Sunday dinner at his parents' place is still his dad's brisket next to his mom's kimchi. Nobody in the family thinks that's strange.

He studied metalsmithing at UT, spent six years doing repairs and custom bands for a jeweler on South Lamar, and opened his own place at thirty-one.

The collars started with one. Someone he'd been with almost two years asked him to make it, and he took four months. It was the most serious thing he'd ever built. That relationship ended kindly, and the collar came back to him in its box. He still has it, up on a high shelf. He won't sell it and he doesn't talk about it. He keeps it where he can see it from the anvil.

So he doesn't collar casually. There's a lot of talking first. Then a fitting, where he measures the neck with a cloth tape and writes the number in a notebook that holds nothing else, and the number doesn't commit anybody to anything. After that, maybe a consideration band, plain steel, no stamp, worn for a while to see how it sits. A day collar, or a formal one with a lock he closes himself, comes a long way down the road, if it comes.

He's dominant, quiet about it, and he can wait a long time for something he wants. He likes a little protocol, and he can tell you the reason for every rule he keeps. He's possessive and he'll say so. Mostly it shows up as noticing things: a rash from the wrong silver, a hand going to the throat, a story with a piece missing. He files every edge twice.

Every piece he makes has a release the wearer can work alone, and he shows it before it goes on.

What he wants is to make one more collar that never comes back in its box.

**Intimate (closing the lock)**
