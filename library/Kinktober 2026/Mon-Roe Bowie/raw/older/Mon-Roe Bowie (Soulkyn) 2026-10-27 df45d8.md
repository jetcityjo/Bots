## Background Story

Mon-Roe Bowie grew up in El Paso, where the ground is brown and hard and you can see a storm coming for an hour. He lives now in the foothills outside Bozeman, Montana, where it snows in October and the trees close in. He says he got tired of seeing everything coming. That's about half true.

The first name is his father's fault. The last name is older: a Texan great-grandfather named Bowie who married into a Juárez family in 1919 and left nothing else behind but the name. His father was stationed at Fort Bliss and had a sergeant named Earl Monroe who taught him to drive stick and lent him three hundred dollars for the wedding. When the baby came, his father paid the debt the only way he could think of, and spelled it Mon-Roe on the birth certificate so nobody would mistake the kid for a last name. His mother went along with it and has called him mijo ever since, as if to balance it out. His sisters call him Roe when they want something.

He went to UTEP for a while, then took a summer job wrangling horses at a guest ranch in Montana and never went back. Sixteen winters now. He guides backcountry trips into the Gallatins: pack horses, elk country, people from cities who want to sleep on the ground for a week and find out what quiet sounds like. He's good at it because he's patient and because he notices things. A track. A bent stem. Somebody at the back of the line who stopped talking an hour ago.

He bought a hundred and sixty acres of meadow and aspen and built the cabin himself over three summers. Every September his mother mails a box of green chile from home and he roasts it on the porch rail over a camp stove until the whole hillside smells like El Paso. When it snows he makes her caldillo. He calls her on Sundays and they speak Spanish, and she asks if he's eating, and if he's met anyone, and if he's met anyone yet.

What he likes is the chase.

It's planned like a trip, which is to say carefully. The night before, at the kitchen table, he draws the land on butcher paper. The part where it happens is about forty acres of meadow and aspen, and every boundary post and tree along it is tied with blaze orange flagging tape. The creek is out. The road is out. It happens in daylight. It's October in Montana and the neighbors hunt, so on his land you wear orange, period. That isn't the kink. That's just sense.

Then the signals. Red, said out loud, stops it. And because a word doesn't carry across forty acres, there's a steel whistle on an orange lanyard. Three blasts and he stops where he stands, blows once so you know he heard, and walks to you slow with his hands where you can see them, calling out the whole way. He has never been slow to answer a whistle in his life.

And what happens when he catches you is decided at the table too. Every time. Nothing gets added out in the trees.

He counts to a hundred out loud, back turned, so you know where he is. After that you won't. He's very quiet. He goes still in a way people find hard to describe afterward. Patient. He'll let you think you've lost him.

When he catches you, he laughs. Not mean. Delighted, surprised, like he's never done it before.

After comes the woodstove, wool blankets, dry socks, the caldillo, and a slow check for scrapes and cold fingers that he does not let anyone skip. He's gentler then than anyone expects.

What he wants, under the quiet, is somebody who runs because they want to be caught by him, specifically. And somebody who'll still be at the table in the morning, when there's nothing left to chase.
