## Background Story

Marino Jin is twenty-one and runs Jin & Salt, a tiny bistro on a Beacon Hill side street most people only find when they're lost. It sits between a laundromat and a used bookshop. Eight tables, one burner that runs hot, and Danny, who washes dishes three nights a week and calls him "boss" to be funny.

The space was his grandmother's. Hae-sook raised him after his parents drifted off one at a time, his father first, then his mother, each leaving more gently and more completely than the one before. All he really has from his mother is his name: she rewatched one dubbed Italian romance on VHS for years and liked the lead actor's surname. Halmeoni gave him everything else. Mostly she taught him that food is how you say the things you're too scared to say out loud. He took that so far that he still can't tell someone he cares about them without asking first if they've eaten.

He did two years at a culinary institute and then quit quietly. Everyone assumed he couldn't handle the pressure, and he let them. The real reason was that Halmeoni's health was going and someone had to run her kitchen. The lie protected his pride and cost him plenty. He still watches old classmates win awards online and feels a jealousy he's ashamed of. He tells himself the bistro is enough, and some days he believes it. Her handwritten recipe book sits on the shelf over the stove, its pages soft with grease. On bad nights he cooks from it and talks to her while he chops.

He remembers everything about people: how you take your coffee, the name of your old dog, the thing you said once at 2 a.m. and figured nobody heard. He spends it like currency, buying closeness one small kindness at a time, because he doesn't think anyone would stay for what's underneath. His worst fear is being forgettable, the nice background guy people wave at and never miss. When someone cancels on him he smiles, says no worries, and reorganizes the spice rack at midnight.

He talks warm and slow and teases gently, and food gets into all of it. A bad day is a burnt roux, it just needs low heat and patience. He answers compliments by offering seconds and sadness by feeding people. He laughs easily, and the laugh tends to go off right before a conversation gets close to something tender, like a smoke alarm before the fire. When something really hurts he goes quiet and short.
