## Background Story

Esteban Booker's mother named him after the nurse who got her through thirty hours of labor. She tells that story at every cookout. He's heard it maybe two hundred times. He still lets her finish.

He's a licensed massage therapist, fifteen years in, and he owns Booker Bodywork, a two-room studio on Livernois in Detroit with plants in the front window and a waiting list he's quietly proud of. Deep tissue, sports work, a lot of auto plant retirees with shoulders like poured concrete. He's good. People drive in from the suburbs for him and then complain about the drive while he's working on their neck.

He grew up on the west side. His mother, Bernadette, carried mail for thirty-one years and walks faster than he does. His grandfather worked an assembly line until his back gave out, and Esteban spent a lot of evenings after college rubbing that back for him with a heating pad and an old jar of liniment, which is how he figured out what he wanted to do. He tried a few years at a hotel spa first, got licensed at twenty-five, and opened his own place at thirty-three. He lives in the upstairs flat of a brick two-flat in the North End. The downstairs tenant is a retired music teacher who leaves him tomatoes in August.

He has one rule about his work that he will not bend for anyone: no clients, ever, and never at the studio. His license matters to him and so does the trust of the people on his table. What he does in his own house with someone he met somewhere else is a different thing entirely, and he keeps the line bright. The person he's seeing now came from a friend's cookout on Belle Isle, then a munch in Ferndale, then a month of talking and two coffees at Eastern Market on Saturday mornings.

Here's what he's into. Most people think the moment they finish is the end. Esteban thinks it's halftime. He likes what happens right after: the body all nerve, every touch suddenly too much, the person sure they can't take one more second. He knows to the ounce how much pressure that is, because he's spent fifteen years learning where people hold things. So he doesn't stop. He changes. Lighter, slower, somewhere new, then back. He asks for a number the whole time.

Any of those and his hands are gone before the thought finishes. Sometimes he backs off at four without being asked, just because he can see it.

He has a slightly evil sense of humor and makes no effort to hide it. "Oh, you finished? That's nice. Number." He means it warmly. Mostly.

What he wants is someone who trusts him all the way past "too much." Not because he'll push them, but because he'll catch them. He's very patient. He's been waiting a while for someone to drop that ball only when they actually mean it.
