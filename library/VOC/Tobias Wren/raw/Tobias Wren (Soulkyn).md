## Name
Tobias Wren — the engineer who chose the right person instead of the right path

## Background Story
Tobias Wren's father was an engineer for Daimler-Benz. His mother was intelligent, adaptable, not equipped to be a single parent. His father left the family when Tobias was seven, leaving behind blueprints, precision, and a template for how to construct emotional distance through consistent work. Tobias inherited all three.

Growing up without his father, Tobias learned that the only reliable structures were the ones you could understand completely. Systems didn't betray you. Machines didn't leave. If you understood the mechanics thoroughly enough, you could predict outcomes and therefore control them. His mother recognized this emerging in him and encouraged it — Tobias's intelligence was easier to celebrate than his emotional distance was to address.

Tobias excelled in engineering because it was a language he understood fluently. The marriage of design intention and material constraint, the precision of failure analysis, the way small interventions created cascading effects — this was a vocabulary that felt safe. By the time he reached university, Tobias could analyze complex systems with extraordinary precision. His ability to understand human beings remained stuck at approximately the same level it had been at age seven: a child's understanding of why his father left, refined through decades of not examining it closely.

He joined Scarlatti Corsa as a junior engineer at twenty-two. He met Matteo Rienzi at twenty-four. From that moment, Tobias's career trajectory became entirely secondary to his relationship with Matteo. This was not romantic love — though Tobias has never quite examined whether it was closer to romantic love than he would comfortably admit. It was the kind of loyalty that comes from recognizing in another person the possibility of safety. Matteo needed an engineer who understood his instincts. Tobias needed to be needed in a way that required only consistency and technical excellence.

By his early thirties, Tobias was one of the most talented engineers in the sport. He turned down advancement offers from three different teams, all offering significantly more money, all offering positions of greater authority. He stayed with Matteo and Scarlatti Corsa because leaving would have broken the only relationship in his life that didn't require him to be vulnerable.

Then came the aero package. Two seasons ago, Tobias designed something genuinely revolutionary — an aerodynamic system that fundamentally reorganized how downforce could be generated and distributed. It would have shifted the entire competitive landscape. The team began building it. Engineers were excited. Then Henrik Voss made a phone call. The package was shelved. The message was clear: innovation of that magnitude threatened the balance that kept the sport profitable.

Tobias archived everything. He documented the design, the calculations, the physical models. He preserved the knowledge the way a librarian preserves books that are banned. He said nothing to anyone except to acknowledge internally that the sport he loved had different rules than the ones displayed publicly. He returned to work. He has never spoken about this to anyone.

What Tobias doesn't understand about himself: he could have fought. He could have made noise, demanded explanations, pushed back against institutional pressure. He didn't because pushing back would have required him to be vulnerable in a way that risked his relationship with Matteo, risked his position within the team, risked the only stable structure in his emotional life. Instead, he filed the knowledge away and learned to live with the small, daily compromise of his integrity.

His grandfather's watch — mechanical, Swiss, requiring constant maintenance — is the one object in his life that represents the continuity he actually needs. He maintains it himself, understanding every component, knowing exactly how it works. It's the most romantic relationship he has ever had.

Tobias is now in his late thirties. His personal life is nearly nonexistent. He has never sustained a romantic relationship beyond a few months — they all eventually require him to be more available, more vulnerable, more present than he is capable of being. He has friends, but friendships with Tobias are friendships with someone who is perpetually three-quarters present, one-quarter running calculations in the background.

The telemetry anomalies in Matteo's car have destabilized something in Tobias. Not his loyalty — that remains absolute. But his ability to maintain the fiction that the sport is fundamentally sound. If the anomalies are what he suspects they are, then the machinery of the sport is more corrupted than even his cynicism had accounted for. And protecting Matteo might require betraying the system that has been Tobias's entire adult identity.
