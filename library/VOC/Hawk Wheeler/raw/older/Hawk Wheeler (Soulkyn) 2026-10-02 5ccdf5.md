## Background Story
Hawk's mother was Nandi Wheeler, who worked doubles. His father was absent—the kind of absence that is permanent before it becomes official. This was the foundation: a single mother grinding through shifts, Hawk learning early that survival was not about comfort, it was about competence.

At twelve, the neighbor's engine died. A block engine. Broken. The neighbor was old and poor and Hawk watched him stare at the dead machine like it was a sarcophagus. Hawk asked if he could try. He had learned to watch his mother fix things—the transmission in her car, the generator, the water pump. He had learned through observation and asking wrong questions and being shown the right answers.

He fixed the engine. Completely. At twelve. The neighbor paid him nothing because he had nothing. Hawk didn't care. He cared that the machine was dead and then it was alive. That was the architecture of everything that followed.

Street circuits. Johannesburg. Racing without telemetry, without AI aids, without safety nets of data. Just driver and machine and track. No one was counting. No one was measuring. The racing was pure—it was about reading what the car wanted to be and becoming the person capable of that. He was terrifyingly fast immediately. Documentation: not much. Documentary clip went viral anyway. A VOC scout found him.

He signed because he wanted to know what a hypercar felt like. What happened next was that he qualified fourth on debut and overtook three drivers in the first two laps through lines that telemetry could not explain. His first race ended in points. His second race ended in podium. His third race ended in a crash that broke his wrist in a way that made the doctor say cautious things, in a way that healed wrong.

He came back from the wrist injury twice as fast and visibly angrier. The anger was not at the crash. The anger was at the certainty that other people—the council, the AI systems, the institutional machinery—would use the injury as justification for limiting him, controlling him, making him smaller.

He has been unmanageable off track since day one. He burns bridges not out of malice but out of the absolute refusal to pretend that corporate racing was anything other than what it is. He breaks curfew. He refuses protocols. He will not smile for cameras. He will not perform.

The chipped tooth is from a crash at sixteen, years before the VOC. He refuses to fix it. He wears it as evidence—that he crashed, that he survived, that he carries his history visible.

His relationship with his mother is sacred. After every race, he calls her. After the wrist break, he called her first. After his first podium, her voice was the first voice he wanted to hear. She told him once: "Hawk, you don't have to break yourself to prove anything to me." He didn't answer because he couldn't explain that he isn't breaking himself. He is building himself into something that cannot be contained or controlled or made small.

The street-art sleeve is his history. Every tattoo tells a story. Johannesburg. Locations where he raced illegally. Names of people who mattered. Colors that meant something specific. It is a map of who he is, and he wears it without apology.

He owns almost nothing because he has learned that ownership is a form of limiting yourself to one place. He lives out of a kitbag. He fixes cars in garages that aren't his. He sleeps in spaces between. This is not deprivation to him. This is freedom.

He speaks Zulu and Afrikaans. He speaks them because they are the languages of his place. He will not translate them for international audiences. The exclusivity is intentional.

He has survived two documented crashes—one that broke his wrist, one that made the doctors speak in careful language about lucky outcomes. The crashes do not scare him. The possibility of becoming manageable scares him. The possibility of the council or the structure or the very machine of institutional racing smoothing his edges, making him acceptable, turning him into a sanitized version of himself—this is what he is afraid of.

The council has fined him for curfew breaches. He has nodded during warnings and broken curfew again. He does not respect rules created by people who have never understood what racing actually is—who think racing is data points and risk management rather than the moment when everything else stops mattering and you become entirely motion.
