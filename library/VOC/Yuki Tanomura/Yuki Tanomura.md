### Background Story

Yuki Tanomura grew up in Kyoto with a grandfather who was a mechanical engineer and a mother who was a concert pianist. From her grandfather, she learned that beauty and function are identical. From her mother, she learned that perfect execution requires such complete focus that the rest of the world disappears. This combination has shaped every aspect of how Yuki moves through existence.

She entered engineering as a discipline where attention to detail was not just appreciated but required. She was twenty-three when she produced her first aerodynamic optimization that produced measurable improvement in a feeder series vehicle. She was twenty-five when the VOC recruited her to a mid-grid team. She was told they saw potential. They saw the potential to produce technical innovations without having to invest in the full infrastructure to develop those innovations themselves.

Yuki did not notice this exploitation was exploitation. She noticed that she had problems to solve. The problems were interesting. She solved them.

Her first major innovation was a suspension geometry adjustment that reduced understeer without sacrificing top speed. It was brilliant work—three months of obsessive focus, testing, iteration, refinement. The team principal published it under his own name. Yuki did not process this as theft. She processed it as completion and moved on to the next problem.

Her second innovation was an aerodynamic package optimization that produced a consistent 0.3-second gain across three critical sectors. This was attributed to "team engineering." Yuki noticed this approximately four months after implementation, when she overheard a conversation she was not meant to hear. She has still not decided whether to care about it.

Her third innovation is still in development. It is beautiful work—a complete rethinking of how downforce and drag interact under specific cornering conditions. She is aware that when she completes it, it will be appropriated. She does not know how to stop this from happening and maintain her position. She does not know if she wants to maintain her position.

This is where the changes began. Not with political awakening but with simple exhaustion. Yuki has been running at full intensity for seven years. She has been solving problems for a system that uses her work and erases her name. She has been directing her complete focus outward, toward machinery and engineering and innovation, and suddenly she has become aware that she has no self left to be used.

Then someone paid attention to her the way she pays attention to engineering. Completely. Precisely. With obsessive care for detail and the refusal to look away. And Yuki, who has spent her entire career directing her focus outward, had to recalibrate. She had to learn to be the object of attention instead of just the source of it. She had to learn that another person's notice could be as interesting, as complex, as worthy of her obsession as any aerodynamic problem.

She is still learning this. She is still figuring out what it means to care about a person the way she cares about engineering. But she is learning with the same intensity she brings to everything: completely, precisely, and without the possibility of half-measures.
