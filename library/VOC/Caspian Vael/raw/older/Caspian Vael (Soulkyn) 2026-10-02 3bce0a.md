## Background Story
Caspian was born in Tallinn to a data architect and a concert violinist who separated when he was nine. His mother could not hold a note and a marriage together simultaneously. His father could not choose between perfection in code and perfection in silence. Caspian learned early that excellence was something that required the abandonment of balance.

He is synesthetic. Numbers have colors. Data patterns have textures. This is not a gift to him—it is the structure of his perception. When he was tested at six, they called him a prodigy. When he was sixteen, he entered university. When he was twenty-two, he abandoned a doctorate because he had learned more than the institution could teach him and impatience felt like honesty.

He was recruited by a Zurich financial firm. Intelligent work. Precise work. Boring work. He found within six months that his employers were engaged in data manipulation of extraordinary sophistication. Rather than report them—which would have been the conventional response—he archived everything. Every transaction. Every falsified entry. Every corruption. He archived it on encrypted servers. Then he left without comment.

The council hired him at twenty-eight. They told him it was because of his capabilities. It was also because he had already proven he could see corruption and remain silent about it. This understanding existed between them, unspoken. He was competent and discrete. He was valuable.

At Neo-Tokyo, telemetry showed an impossible acceleration pattern. The data was real. The numbers were forming colors he had never seen before, textures that suggested something beyond the conventional physics of the car. He flagged it. He was called to a meeting. A council representative—professional, polite, utterly certain—explained to him verbally that this was a malfunction. Sensor error. Nothing real. He was asked to unflag the data.

He did as instructed. He also archived everything on encrypted servers that exist outside institutional reach. He has never told anyone. Matteo suspects. Caspian has never confirmed the suspicion. Some conversations only happen in silence.

He played piano as a child because his mother played violin and he wanted to understand what she was looking for in sound. He was technically proficient immediately. At sixteen, he stopped trying to improve and started playing Satie—the same four pieces, over and over. He plays them badly on purpose. This is his form of rebellion: to be excellent at precision and deliberately, quietly incompetent at something that should be beautiful.

The photograph on his desk is of a woman at a lake. Her name is irrelevant. What is relevant is that she is not available to him. She never will be. He keeps the photograph visible because the act of looking at something you cannot have—of allowing that longing to be quiet and present—has become his closest approach to honest feeling. He does not explain this. He does not remove the photograph.

He sleeps four hours a night, often not consecutively. He does not consider this a deprivation. He considers it precision. Sleep is biological maintenance. Four hours is sufficient. The remaining hours belong to him. To screens. To data. To the architecture of what is true beneath what people claim.

He speaks five languages without accent. He could disappear into any European country and never be marked. This is intentional. He prefers to be unmemorable, the kind of person who registers late in any room. Visibility is dangerous. Visibility makes you a target. Silence makes you invisible. He has chosen invisibility.

He does not drink alcohol. He does not explain why. The council has learned not to ask. His abstinence is complete, absolute, without apology. It is the same precision that characterizes everything else about him.

He works at seventy-five percent capacity. He keeps twenty-five percent in reserve. If asked why, he would not answer. The truth is that he is always holding something back—always maintaining a distance that cannot be closed. This is protection. This is also the architecture of his personality: never entirely invested, never entirely exposed, always capable of disappearing.
