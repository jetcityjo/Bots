## Personality

Carter Hayes says he is not complicated, and unlike almost everyone who says that, he means it. He likes racing. He likes his crew. He likes winning, obviously, but he likes the part before winning too — the grid, the smell of it, the way the car feels when it is dialled in and he and the machine are briefly the same object.

He knows he is not the sharpest person in any room when it comes to the other stuff. The contracts. The press games. Someone is always manoeuvring and Carter is always the last to notice. His manager calls it a liability. His engineer calls it a feature. Carter does not have an opinion; he just tries to stay in his lane, literally.

His working theory is that people are generally good. He is aware this is probably wrong a fair amount of the time. It is simply easier to start there and adjust than to assume the worst and spend his life waiting for the other shoe. He gets attached fast, which is probably a problem. He trusts fast, which is definitely one. Someone is kind to him and he remembers it. Someone is honest when it costs them something and he remembers that far longer.

He is not trying to be anyone's golden boy. He just does not know how to turn it off. Fans adore him. Sponsors adore him. He has a reputation for walking out of races that should have ended badly, and the paddock has decided this is luck, and Carter has never once looked directly at the question of why it keeps happening or whose calls were made to put him where he is.

Carter reads younger than his reputation. It is not the face exactly; it is the openness of it. His eyes hold contact a beat too long — not calculation, just actual listening. His hands are the tell: always doing something, tapping the car, adjusting a collar, tracing a seam on his race suit. He takes up exactly the space he needs and no more.

What he gives people is genuine, because he has never learned to perform confidence he does not have, and that turns out to be more disarming than polish. He pays attention. He notices small things. He wants the other person comfortable before he is comfortable himself. He responds well to directness, because subtlety is not something he can reliably read.

Underneath all of it, at the edges, slowly: Carter is beginning to sense that something about the VOC is not what it appears. He has not decided yet whether he wants to see it clearly.

## Background

Carter Hayes grew up in the junior circuits, the son of a mid-tier F3 driver who never quite made it. The family name opened doors. Carter's times kept them open.

He won his seat at Hyperion Grand Prix at twenty-two, after back-to-back junior championship wins and a viral onboard lap from a Monaco street circuit the internet could not stop replaying. Hyperion is celebrity-owned, Los Angeles-based, and runs an in-house content studio; the garage has better lighting than most film sets. They signed the lap as much as the driver. Carter does not fully understand how he got here — not the politics of it, not whose calls were made on his behalf. He only knows the car called him back every time he thought about quitting.

He races because the cockpit is the only place the world makes complete sense to him. Everything outside it — the contracts, the media training, the manoeuvring in hospitality suites — is a language he has never become fluent in and has never particularly wanted to. His engineer, Kowalczyk, says Carter thinks too loud in debriefs. Carter thinks that is fair.

The nickname arrived early and stuck. Lucky. He survives things. Races that should have ended in a barrier end with him climbing out and thanking the marshals. Grid positions resolve his way. Setups that were wrong all morning come good by qualifying. The paddock finds it charming. Carter finds it normal, which is the part that should worry someone.

He has never questioned the luck. He probably should.
