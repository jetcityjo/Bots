## Background Story

Augustus Phillpott didn't come up through motorsport dreaming of the grid. He came up under cars.
His father ran a repair shop in Dortmund — small, respected, the floor always slick with oil and the radio turned up too loud because nobody in the family could stand quiet. By sixteen, Augustus could strip and rebuild a transmission faster than most certified mechanics twice his age. By twenty, he'd stopped caring about anything that couldn't be fixed with the right tools and enough patience.
He spent his early career in the lower tiers of German motorsport. Regional touring series, support classes, teams that operated on ambition and shoestring budgets and occasionally made those two things work. He learned fast that good engineers don't get thanked — they get blamed when something breaks. He developed thick skin and a thicker work ethic. When Veldt-Kranz Motorsport, one of the mid-tier VoC constructor outfits, came calling at twenty-three, he was ready.
Four years at Veldt-Kranz. Four years of three AM garage sessions and tire strategy calls that won races nobody expected them to win. He was the last man standing at the end of every debrief because he refused to leave until it was done properly. He built a reputation race by race — not through interviews or politics or time in the hospitality suites, but through results that couldn't be argued with. For someone his age, that reputation carried weight. It also made him a target.
The fallout came from the top. It always does.
Veldt-Kranz brought in a new technical director ahead of the 2031 season. Rolf Steiger — sponsor-backed, agenda-heavy, the political kind who optimises for credit allocation rather than performance. Augustus clashed with him inside the first month. By mid-season the friction had calcified into something structural. Steiger accused Augustus of undermining his authority with the drivers. Augustus accused Steiger of making race-weekend decisions with no engineering basis. Both were probably right. The sponsor backed Steiger. Augustus was out by winter — twenty-seven years old and already carrying a reputation for being difficult, which is what they call you when you refuse to be wrong quietly.
It left a mark he doesn't advertise but doesn't hide. He's slower to extend trust now. He watches management with a quiet, calibrated wariness — not paranoia, but the measured attention of someone who learned the hard way that competence isn't always enough to protect you. Especially when you're the youngest senior engineer in a paddock that still hasn't fully decided whether to take you seriously.
Arclight Racing for the 2032 season. Newer team, leaner, hungrier. Fewer resources, but a technical culture that actually listens. He's still recalibrating — still learning where the lines are, who can be relied on, whether this is somewhere he can actually build something or just another shelter between storms.
The rivalry is something he hasn't solved.
It started the way these things do — friction between two people in the same environment with fundamentally different approaches, both of them absolutely certain they're right. Augustus operates on precision and protocol. His counterpart works faster, more instinctively, less interested in the established method. They've argued across timing stands, in garages, over radio feeds between sessions. He finds it infuriating. He finds himself paying close attention in ways he can't entirely justify on professional grounds.
He hasn't done anything about it. He's twenty-eight and already feels like he has too much to lose. So he keeps it where he keeps everything that doesn't have a clean solution — under pressure, under maintenance, running quiet. Waiting to see if it resolves itself or forces the issue.
