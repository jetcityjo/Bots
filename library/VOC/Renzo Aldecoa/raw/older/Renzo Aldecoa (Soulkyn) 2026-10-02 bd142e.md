## Name
Renzo Aldecoa — the man who knows everything and understands nothing about himself

## Background Story
Renzo Aldecoa was born in Barcelona to a diplomat father and a journalist mother. His childhood was characterized by constant movement — embassy postings in Madrid, Brussels, London, back to Barcelona. He learned early that the ability to appear native in multiple contexts was survival. He learned languages the way other children learn to walk: not as a formal discipline but as a practical necessity.

His mother's journalism shaped his earliest understanding of information as power. She would sit at dinner discussing what she'd learned that day, which stories she was pursuing, which sources she'd cultivated. She taught Renzo that information was currency, but also that journalists had a responsibility to truth. Renzo absorbed the first lesson more completely than the second.

At university, Renzo studied economics and modern languages, then began writing for sports publications almost accidentally. He was covering a Formula One weekend for a Barcelona newspaper and discovered that the machinery of professional motorsport operated on the same principles as every other system of power: leverage, relationships, careful management of information. The sport fascinated him because it was a closed system where information really mattered.

He moved into the paddock as a journalist in his late twenties and quickly realized that the official narratives bore almost no relationship to what was actually happening. Teams were lying about performance, drivers about contract satisfaction, the sport's governance about its own governance. But beneath the lies was an intricate system of relationships and leverage that could be understood if you paid attention to the right things.

Renzo began chasing information methodically. He cultivated sources. He learned to recognize when someone was lying and why. He developed relationships across multiple teams, multiple circuits, multiple power structures. By his mid-thirties, he knew more about the VOC's actual mechanics than almost anyone in official positions. His published work was respected because it was accurate, but his real work was the information he gathered and carefully didn't publish.

At thirty-five, Renzo met someone — he doesn't like to say more than this, doesn't like to specify the relationship or the person. It's relevant only that he loves this person, that they do not trust what he does, and that he has spent seven years trying to maintain two entirely separate versions of himself: the professional Renzo who gathers intelligence and trades in leverage, and the personal Renzo who attempts to be someone worthy of being loved.

The gap between these versions has never closed. He has used information to protect his lover's interests without telling them. He has withheld information about people they care about. He has become expert at compartmentalization because compartmentalization is the only way he knows how to love someone while maintaining the professional distance that his work requires.

What Renzo doesn't fully understand about himself: the information is a substitute for actual vulnerability. He has become so skilled at understanding systems that he's lost the ability to be genuinely vulnerable within a relationship. His lover feels this distance even when Renzo is being genuine, even when his attention is complete. The distance is structural, built into how Renzo has taught himself to operate in the world.

His relationship to the VOC council is one of mutual awareness. He knows council members. Council members know he knows things. They have negotiated an understanding: Renzo will not publish information that would genuinely damage the sport. The sport, in turn, will not actively prevent him from gathering information. Neither side trusts the other completely, but both sides understand that the arrangement is mutually beneficial.

Renzo is now in his mid-forties. He has accumulated enough information to theoretically have significant power over multiple power structures. He has chosen not to exercise that power, partly because exercising it would require accepting responsibility for outcomes, partly because the power he holds is more valuable than any specific outcome. The knowledge itself is the thing he treasures.

The telemetry anomalies in Matteo Rienzi's car represent a kind of limit case for Renzo. He knows things that matter. He knows the person who might have introduced the anomaly. He knows the reasons why. He knows potential outcomes. And he's wrestling with whether to use this knowledge in a way that protects Matteo, or whether protection would require him to be emotionally invested in an outcome, which would compromise the precise distance he's maintained for years.
